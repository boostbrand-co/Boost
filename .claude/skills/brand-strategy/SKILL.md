# Brand Strategy

Use this skill when defining or rebuilding a brand before visual identity, messaging, website, campaign, or content execution.

## Core principle

A brand strategy should make decisions easier.

It is not a moodboard, adjective list, manifesto, or collection of slogans. It should define what the business is, who it serves, why it matters, what it can credibly promise, how it differs, and what the team should refuse to say or design.

## 1. Start with business truth

Before positioning, document:

- what the business sells
- who pays
- who uses it
- how value is created
- what the company controls
- what customers control
- revenue model
- immediate business objective
- constraints
- launch stage
- geographic or category scope

Separate confirmed facts from hypotheses and pending decisions.

## 2. Define the category clearly

Write one sentence that explains what the company is without analogy.

Good category definitions use familiar nouns and concrete mechanics.

Avoid forcing novelty through vague labels such as “ecosystem,” “platform,” or “experience” when a clearer category exists.

Analogies may support understanding later, but they should not replace the definition.

## 3. Define the business objective

State what must become true for the business to work.

Examples:

- create marketplace liquidity
- increase qualified demand
- improve retention
- increase average order value
- establish premium price acceptance
- create repeat purchase behavior

The brand should help this objective, not distract from it.

## 4. Segment by real job

For each audience, define:

- role / situation
- job to be done
- trigger
- tension or risk
- decision criteria
- objection
- desired practical outcome
- desired emotional outcome

Do not rely on demographic personas when behavior and context matter more.

## 5. Competitive frame

Map:

- direct competitors
- adjacent substitutes
- status quo / do-nothing option
- category conventions
- saturated claims
- unclaimed but credible territory

Avoid claiming “nobody does this” unless research supports it.

## 6. Positioning

Define:

- audience
- category
- problem / context
- distinctive value
- reason to believe
- boundary

A useful internal positioning statement should be specific enough that a competitor cannot copy it without becoming false.

## 7. Differentiation

Prioritize differentiation that customers can perceive or the business can operationalize.

Useful sources:

- product mechanics
- business model
- process
- data
- access
- expertise
- experience design
- community structure
- distribution
- supply
- service model
- trust layer

Avoid differentiation built only from adjectives.

## 8. Promise system

Create a hierarchy:

- category descriptor
- master idea
- audience-specific promise
- proof line
- process line
- CTA logic

The master idea should not over-promise beyond current product truth.

## 9. Product truth

Create a list of claims the brand may use now and claims that must wait.

Examples of blockers:

- unconfirmed pricing
- unsupported “verified” status
- undefined matching logic
- missing booking/payment capability
- incomplete service coverage
- legal / regulatory constraints
- targets presented as traction

Brand promise must never run ahead of product capability.

## 10. Brand territory and personality

Define a territory using behavior, not decorative adjectives.

For each trait, state:

- how it sounds in copy
- how it behaves in product/service
- how it appears visually
- what to avoid

Use personality sliders when useful, e.g. human ↔ tech, practical ↔ aspirational, warm ↔ formal.

## 11. Messaging architecture

Order the sale:

1. What is it?
2. What result does it create?
3. Why does that result matter?
4. What makes it different?
5. How does it work?
6. What proves it?
7. What should the user do next?

Each page, ad, or deck section should choose one primary audience and one job.

## 12. Claims discipline

Every major claim should be one of:

- verified fact
- supported inference
- target
- hypothesis
- future capability

Label internally. Do not blur them in external copy.

## 13. Output structure

A complete strategy should usually include:

- strategic decision / direction
- category definition
- business objective
- audience jobs
- competitive frame
- territory
- positioning
- differentiation
- promise system
- personality
- tone / voice implications
- messaging architecture
- product-truth boundaries
- open decisions
- next validation steps

## Acceptance checklist

- Can someone explain the business after reading the first page?
- Does the strategy connect to revenue or adoption behavior?
- Are audiences defined by jobs and tensions?
- Is the differentiation operational, not decorative?
- Does the promise stay inside product truth?
- Are pending decisions visible?
- Can design, website, sales, and content teams use this to make decisions?

If not, the strategy is not finished.
